# ICS 591 Prosit 2: data pipeline questions, answered from TriageLens

Study notes on the Prosit 2 questions, using what TriageLens actually did. I read `s` as the number of paragraphs, because question 1 says "s = 1 paragraph". An equation or worked sum belongs to the paragraph around it.

Paths in code style that start with `ml/`, and ADR-13 to ADR-18, are on the branch `origin/ml/E11-01-montgomery-download`. They are not on `main` yet. The last section lists the sources and what could not be checked.

## 1. What is a data pipeline, and why does it fail most often?

A data pipeline is the chain of steps that turns raw data into something a model can learn from or score: collect, check, label, split, transform, version, serve and monitor. In TriageLens that chain starts with `ml/pipeline/build_dataset.py`, which reads the Shenzhen images and writes a manifest. It ends in the phone's worker, which must turn a new photo into the same pixels the model saw in training. The pipeline is the part most likely to fail for three reasons. It depends on things the team does not control, such as a source that changes its format. It usually fails silently, because bad data still produces a neat number and raises no error. It also crosses every boundary in the project, from Python to the browser and from one owner to another, so nobody sees all of it. Our own example: image shape alone predicted TB in Shenzhen because the provider cropped TB films narrower. Nothing crashed, and a logistic regression on image size reached validation AUROC 0.801 (95% CI 0.706 to 0.885) with no pixels at all (ADR-16). Sambasivan et al. (2021) call this kind of delayed, compounding damage "data cascades", and 92% of the 53 AI practitioners they interviewed had met at least one. That shows the problem is common. It does not show that the pipeline fails more often than every other component, and I found no measured rate that proves "most often".

## 2. How do you source data when little of it exists?

Start with what exists, and be honest about whom it represents. **Existing datasets** are the cheapest and safest legally, and they are our core build (TRD 12, Appendix A). The trade-off is fit: Shenzhen is one hospital, one country and one machine type, with no African patients and about two thirds men, so results may not hold in Ghana (hazard H4). "Public" also does not mean free of terms. The Shenzhen ReadMe says not to share the data outside the research group, which is why we commit only IDs, labels and hashes (ML-DATA-07). **Partner data** gives the best fit, because it could come from Ghanaian clinics and machines. It carries the highest cost: ethics approval, a data-sharing agreement, de-identification and Act 843 duties. That is why it is the stretch story `E15-02` and can never block delivery.

**Scraping** radiology teaching sites or image search looks quick, but it is the weakest option. The people in the images did not consent, licences are often unclear, labels are unverified and the images skew toward striking teaching cases. We do not use it. **Instrumentation** means collecting data from the running system. It gives the truest picture of real use, including real phones and real lighting. It also makes us a data controller of health data, and early on it has no verified labels. So ADR-10 keeps results-only as the default and uploads images only with consent, and the slice stores numbers only.

**Synthesis** creates new examples, for example the phone-photo simulation of glare, tilt and moire (ML-ROBUST-01) and the corruptions for the gate model. It raises fewer privacy issues and can fill rare conditions, but it teaches the model the simulator's idea of the world, so it must be tested on real data. That is why we also need 300 or more real phone photos of displayed or printed X-rays (ML-ROBUST-02). **Augmentation** varies existing images, and it is cheap, but it adds no new patients or sites and can destroy signal. We ban horizontal flips for chest X-rays because they would put the heart on the wrong side. We also refused blur and noise as a fix for the texture finding, because they could remove real disease signal and Shenzhen could not show which was removed (ADR-16). Neither synthesis nor augmentation fixes who is missing from the data.

## 3. How do you obtain labels, and how do you know they are good?

Labels can come from existing records, expert annotators, crowd workers, rules that read reports, or later confirmation such as a lab test. Each one measures something slightly different, so first ask what the label really means. Shenzhen's file label 1 means "abnormal lung", not TB, so `ml/pipeline/build_dataset.py` also requires a TB term in the written reading. That check quarantined 18 TB-labelled records in version 2. It also shows a limit of rules: 16 of the 18 were probably excluded only because the word list did not recognise `STB`, not because the reading was in doubt (Shenzhen v2 data card). A rule checked against one reading is a consistency check, not agreement between readers. We do not yet have a second reader or a lab result for these labels, so we have not measured their reliability.

Inter-annotator agreement measures how far independent labellers give the same label to the same items. Raw percentage agreement misleads when one class is common, because two people can agree often just by both choosing that class. Here is an invented example, not project data, for 100 films read by readers A and B: both say TB on 2, only A says TB on 4, only B says TB on 4, and both say normal on 90. Raw agreement looks excellent at $p_o = 0.92$. Each reader says TB 6% of the time, so chance alone gives $p_e = (0.06)(0.06) + (0.94)(0.94) = 0.8872$. Cohen's kappa measures how much of the possible improvement over chance was achieved:

$$\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e} = \frac{0.92 - 0.8872}{1 - 0.8872} \approx 0.29$$

So 92% agreement becomes a weak 0.29. The reason is easy to see: 10 films got a TB call from at least one reader, and the readers agreed on only 2 of those 10. For more than two readers use Fleiss' kappa or Krippendorff's alpha. For ordered grades, such as the five DR grades, use quadratic weighted kappa, which punishes a grade 0 against grade 4 disagreement more than grade 1 against grade 2 (`E3-02`).

I would use kappa of at least 0.6 as a minimum for training labels and aim for at least 0.8 for the reference labels in a test set. These cut-offs are my judgement, not laws. Landis and Koch (1977) label 0.41 to 0.60 moderate, 0.61 to 0.80 substantial and 0.81 to 1.00 almost perfect, and they themselves call the divisions "clearly arbitrary". The reason for being stricter on test labels is that their errors limit what you can measure: the model is graded against them, so noise there makes every reported figure unreliable. Training can tolerate some noise. I would also report the confidence interval, check agreement per class and look at the actual disagreements. For TriageLens this raises a warning about our own plan. FR-10 stores a raw reviewer "agreement rate", which will look high whenever most cases fall in one zone. Reviewers also see the AI result first, so they are not independent and may simply follow it (hazard H2). The dashboard should not present that figure as label quality.

## 4. What does it mean to version a dataset?

Versioning a dataset means that an exact set of records, labels and splits has a permanent name you can point to, so that "the data" always means one thing. Git alone is not the answer. It handles large binary images badly, it would keep every image forever in history, and for us it would break the rules, because the repository is public and the Shenzhen terms forbid sharing the images. So we version the description and keep the bytes elsewhere: the committed `manifest.csv` holds file names, labels, splits and SHA-256 and dHash fingerprints, and `dataset_version_hash` (`0445b087fed038c4` for Shenzhen v2) changes if any file, byte or split changes. The hash also stops false comparisons. v1 and v2 differ by 17 records, so results on them are not directly comparable. The three related ideas are different. **Provenance** is where the data came from and on what terms. **Lineage** is every step that turned it into this version. **Reproducibility** means someone else can follow that record and get the same result.

To rebuild our training set exactly, a person needs six things: the raw 662 Shenzhen images from NLM with bytes matching the recorded SHA-256 values; the clinical readings; the pipeline at the recorded version (0.2.1, commit `b21a47f`) with pinned dependencies, including Pillow; seed 591; the v1 manifest, because v2 carries its splits over from v1 rather than drawing new ones; and the preprocessing version, `cxr-pre-2`. The test is simple: run `build_dataset.py` and check that the hash matches. Each training run should log that hash, the code commit, config and seed (TRD 9.4). There is one real weakness. A hash proves that a file is the same, but it cannot bring back a file that is gone. If NLM changes or removes the data, the manifest would detect it but could not restore it, so the team's private copy matters. The Montgomery images have a weaker origin, because they come from a third-party Kaggle copy, and their first-download hashes only prove that they have not changed since that download.

## 5. What is data validation, where does it belong, and what happens on failure?

Data validation checks data against written expectations before anything relies on it. It belongs at every boundary, not in one place: at ingest, after labelling, before splitting, before training, at the phone's input and at the database. Failing early is cheap, while failing after training costs GPU hours and may never be noticed. The right response depends on how far the problem spreads. A bad single record goes to quarantine with a logged reason, and "no record is dropped silently". A problem with the whole batch is a hard failure that stops the run and writes nothing, for example a patient appearing in two splits, or fewer than 300 records kept (`ml/pipeline/README.md`). At serving time, the safe failure is to give no answer. A failed quality check gives a retake reason and no zone, and a model whose hash differs from the thresholds file produces no results (TRD 7.2, ADR-20).

The three kinds of error differ in how visible they are. **Schema violations** break the agreed format, such as a file name that does not match `CHNCXR_####_[01].png`, an image that does not decode, an unknown field sent to `save_case` or an age band the database does not allow. These are fully automatic and should block. **Distribution anomalies** are valid records that look unusual as a group, such as a TB rate more than 5 points from the overall rate in a split, or a live PSI that climbs. A machine can detect these, but it cannot explain them, so a person must decide what they mean. An early run shows this well: a 64-bit dHash chained 416 of 662 X-rays into one near-duplicate group. The TB-rate check failed and stopped the run, which revealed a bug in our method, not in the data.

**Semantic errors** are correct in format and normal in distribution, but wrong in meaning. A film labelled TB may in fact have some other abnormality, or a grey image that is not a chest X-ray may pass every check. Our quality v1 rules let such an image through and it can receive a zone, which is why the slice is limited to signed-in team accounts until the learned gate exists (H3, ADR-19). Automatic checks catch only the semantic errors someone predicted, like a label that contradicts its reading. The rest need people: clinical readers, the review of the 20 worst failures per model (ML-EVAL-04) and testing on a different site. So schema checks can be fully automatic, distribution checks can raise alerts automatically, and semantic errors mostly cannot be caught automatically. The 2 quarantined Shenzhen readings that need a radiologist are still waiting, because nobody on the team can review them.

## 6. How should data be split, and how does leakage happen?

Each split has one job. Training fits the weights. Validation chooses things such as the epoch, the backbone, the temperature and the thresholds. The test set estimates performance once, after everything is fixed. Shenzhen v2 has 431 training, 94 validation and 93 held-out images, split by patient with a fixed seed. Honest naming matters too: we relabelled the 93-image split as a second held-out validation set, because the same site and the same small pool cannot show how the model generalises. Only Montgomery, from a different country and hospital, can do that (ADR-18). Leakage is any route by which information from the evaluation data, or information unavailable in real use, reaches the model or its choices. It makes results look better than they will be in the field.

**Entity leakage** happens when one person or object appears on both sides, such as two films from the same patient, so the model is partly recognising the person. We assign splits by patient and also join records linked by a near-duplicate dHash, and the pipeline hard-fails on any overlap. Dataset overlap counts too: RSNA is derived from NIH data, and TBX11K must be checked against Shenzhen and Montgomery (TRD Appendix A). **Group leakage** happens when a shared factor, such as a hospital, machine or batch, sits on both sides and is linked to the label. The model then learns the site rather than the disease, and a same-site split will not reveal it. We saw a form of this: padding would have turned the provider's cropping into black bars that revealed the label (H11). Hold out whole sites to test for this. **Temporal leakage** happens when training uses data from after the moment of prediction, or features that would not yet exist, so old data is effectively predicting the past. The data cards for our public sets record no capture dates, so we cannot run a time-based test. That is a gap: once live data exists, train on earlier data and test on later data.

A random split by item is the wrong choice whenever items are not independent or the deployment differs from the data: repeat films from one patient, several sites, data that changes over time, or very rare classes (use stratified splits for those). It works only when each row really is an independent draw from the same conditions you will deploy in. Leakage can also come through process rather than rows. Calibrating int8 ranges on the images that then judge the int8 drop would flatter it, so ADR-17 calibrates on training images instead. Choosing the best seed would flatter the score, so ADR-18 fixed in advance that the median seed is frozen. Thresholds come from validation only, and each locked set is scored once per candidate (ML-DATA-04). `evaluate.py` records a claim on a set before reading it, so a retry can never score it twice.

## 7. What is training and serving skew?

Training and serving skew is a difference between what the model saw in training and what it receives in use, so the same image gives a different score even though the weights have not changed. A concrete mechanism from our app: training used Pillow's bilinear resize, which widens its filter when shrinking, while a browser canvas resize does not. A 3,000-pixel X-ray would therefore reach the model with different fine texture, and ADR-16 found that the model's score follows edge energy strongly, so this could shift the scores in the app with no error and no change to the model. Skew also comes from changing the serving format (int8 lost 0.029 AUROC on Montgomery, so fp32 ships for now), from configuration, such as thresholds set for a different file, and from the world itself, since training used clean digital PNGs while the app receives phone photos of films.

We detect and prevent it at three levels. Before release, `apps/pwa/src/lib/preprocess.ts` ports Pillow's maths and a fixture test checks grayscale conversion and resizing byte for byte against Pillow output. At load, the app compares the model's SHA-256 against the thresholds file and refuses results on a mismatch. In production, each record stores the preprocessing version, the monitoring page counts mismatches, and PSI compares live input statistics with the training reference. These have limits. The version count only compares a label, so a code change that kept the old label would go unseen. The fixture starts from decoded pixels, so the browser's image decoding is not covered by it. The strongest test would be a golden check, as TRD 9.5 asks: run fixed images through the deployed browser path and compare the final model output against the Python reference.

## 8. How do you choose a model from your data and compute, not benchmarks?

Benchmark rankings come from other people's data and hardware, so start from your own limits. Ours are hard: 431 labelled training images for TB, about 80 GPU hours, a 15 MB model, a 2.5 s p95 on a 2 GB phone, and WebAssembly on a CPU. Those limits rule out large networks before any accuracy score is seen, and they favour transfer learning from ImageNet weights on a small backbone. They also shape the selection rule, which ADR-15 fixed in order: validation AUROC, then the AUROC lost in int8, then size. Validation AUROC ended in a tie, because the 0.012 gap (EfficientNet-Lite0 0.924, sd 0.014, against MobileNetV3-Large 0.912, sd 0.008) was smaller than the spread between seeds. Deployment facts then settled it. EfficientNet-Lite0 lost about half as much in int8 and kept just 1 float operation against 37. Neither passed the int8 rule, so we ship EfficientNet-Lite0 at full precision (12.8 MB), which still fits the budget (ADR-18). The plan's default backbone lost, and a benchmark would not have told us that.

A baseline is the simplest thing that could work, and it sets the bar a complex model must clear. Our ladder has three rungs: image size only, logistic regression on 64 by 64 pixels, and a frozen backbone with logistic regression (`ml/train/baselines.py`). When a simple baseline lands within a few points of a complex model, there are three likely explanations, and each calls for a different response. The problem may really be simple, so ship the simple model, since it is cheaper, easier to explain and easier to run. The data may have hit a ceiling set by label noise or sample size, so get better data rather than a bigger model. Or both models may have found the same artefact. Our rung 0 shows the third case. It uses no image content and still scored 0.801 (95% CI 0.706 to 0.885, Shenzhen v1 validation), against 0.922 for the frozen model (Shenzhen v2 validation). Those are different dataset versions, so the comparison is only rough. That baseline is a warning about the dataset, not a rival model, and it told us that even the 0.922 needed outside testing.

Baselines also tell you what to do next. Compare the three seeds before believing a gap, since a gap inside the spread is not a result. Then check the choice on the deployment target and a different site. Montgomery gave AUROC 0.887 (95% CI 0.830 to 0.938), but at the validation threshold of probability 0.184 sensitivity was 0.702 (95% CI 0.573 to 0.805), below the 85% floor. So for this project a better threshold and calibration per site matter more right now than a fancier network.

## 9. What is involved in integrating a learned component?

The model needs a narrow, versioned contract, and the rest of the system should not know how it works inside. Our **inference interface** is a Web Worker with two requests, `init` and `screen`. It replies with `ready`, `screened` or a named error such as `model_checksum` or `model_run` (`apps/pwa/src/inference/messages.ts`). The model's own contract is a float32 `image` input of [1, 3, 320, 320], with `tb_logit` and `features` as outputs. The triage rules sit outside the model, so the score and the clinical decision are separate and can be tested separately. **Batch against online:** batch prediction scores many items ahead of time, which suits work where nobody is waiting. Online prediction answers one request as it happens. We need online, because a health worker is standing with a patient, and it runs on the phone, because clinics may have no internet. Batch still has a place, in evaluation runs, and later in syncing saved results in small groups (TRD 10.8).

**Budgets** turn "fast enough" into something testable: p95 inference of at most 2.5 s, capture to result of at most 5 s, the quality gate at most 300 ms, peak memory at most 350 MB and no main-thread task over 50 ms (TRD 8). For us, throughput is one person at a time, so memory and tail latency matter far more than requests per second. Decoding and inference stay in a worker to keep the screen responsive. The plan for devices with 2 GB or less is one thread and one model at a time (TRD 10.5). **Registry and versioning** answer the question "which exact model produced this result?". Every record stores the model, threshold and preprocessing versions, the quality scores and the model hash (FR-12). The full plan is signed manifests, background updates and rollback (`E9-04`, ADR-11). The slice has less than that: a fixed run ID, a hash check and an unsigned thresholds file. The `model_versions` table is not yet in the migration, and `E9-04` is third in the cut order, so we should say this plainly rather than claim a registry exists.

**When the component is unavailable,** the system must fail toward safety, and it must be obvious. If the model cannot download, fails its hash check or is paired with thresholds that are pending, the app shows no zone at all. It never falls back to a guess or a default answer, because a false "Low risk" is the worst outcome here (H1). The worker also uses one thread if the page is not cross-origin isolated, so it runs more slowly rather than not at all. The reverse failure matters too: if the database is down, the screening result still shows and the screen says it was not saved. In the slice that record is then lost, because the offline queue was left out on purpose. The planned design writes each case locally first and sends it later through an outbox that is safe to repeat (TRD 10.8), and the core flow must work with no network (ADR-7). A reviewer check, the "Not sure" zone and "A clinician decides" are all part of the same fallback: the human remains the safety net.

## 10. What is a thin vertical slice, and why deploy one early?

A thin vertical slice is one narrow path that runs through every layer in the real environment, even if each layer does very little. Ours goes from sign-in and age band, through photo and quality check, to the TB model on the phone, then a zone and a saved numbers-only record that shows on a monitoring page (ADR-19). It is deliberately incomplete: no pneumonia, no eye check, no offline queue and no learned gate. You deploy it before it is good because integration risk is the risk you cannot reason away. The model was not good enough yet, since external sensitivity was 0.702 at its validation threshold, but the slice's purpose is to prove that the path works and to expose what fails only when the parts meet.

Building components in parallel and integrating at the end feels efficient, because each person gets local progress. But it hides the hard problems until the time left to fix them is smallest, and every interface stays an untested assumption. Our slice forced those problems out early: cross-origin isolation headers blocked the model URL unless it had CORS and CORP headers, a canvas resize would not match Pillow, thresholds had to be tied to one exact model file, and an accepted risk (grey non-chest images) became visible. It also produced monitoring data sooner. The cost is real and was recorded: the slice broke the Sprint 0 capacity cap and the swap rule, and its thresholds were not calibrated (ADR-19, ADR-20). That was a one-off for a fixed deadline, and these thin parts still have to be finished in Sprint 1.

## 11. What can be monitored at the data layer, and how would you detect drift?

Without any labels you can still monitor a lot: input statistics, the share of failed quality checks, the score distribution, the zone mix, latency and the versions in use. Our monitoring page tracks these and stores no images (`apps/pwa/src/lib/monitor.ts`). It covers aspect ratio, edge energy, mean brightness and width. Edge energy is there on purpose, since ADR-16 showed that scores follow it. With labels, or a proxy, you can add reviewer agreement. Three kinds of drift need separate treatment. **Covariate shift** means $P(x)$ changes while $P(y \mid x)$ stays the same, as when a new X-ray machine or phone camera changes the images. **Label shift** means $P(y)$ changes while $P(x \mid y)$ stays the same, as when TB prevalence differs. **Concept drift** means $P(y \mid x)$ itself changes, so the same image should now get a different label, for example after a change in the reference test or in what counts as referable.

Label shift is already built into our deployment. Validation is about 52% TB, while a clinic will have far less. Take the refer threshold on Shenzhen v2 validation, with sensitivity 0.469 (95% CI 0.337 to 0.606) and specificity 0.956 (95% CI 0.852 to 0.988), and assume a prevalence of 5%, the kind of assumption ML-EVAL-01 asks us to report. Then the share of Refer now results that are true TB is $\frac{0.469 \times 0.05}{0.469 \times 0.05 + 0.044 \times 0.95} \approx 0.36$, even though the model has not changed. For detection, our page uses the Population Stability Index. Divide a statistic into bins using the training reference, then compare the share of live values $a_i$ with the share of reference values $b_i$ in each bin:

$$\mathrm{PSI} = \sum_{i=1}^{B} (a_i - b_i)\ln\frac{a_i}{b_i}$$

Empty bins are floored at $10^{-4}$ so the logarithm stays defined (`apps/pwa/src/lib/stats.ts`). For example, if the reference shares are (0.5, 0.3, 0.2) and the live shares are (0.2, 0.3, 0.5), PSI is $0.275 + 0 + 0.275 = 0.55$. The usual rule of thumb, attributed to Lewis (1994), reads under 0.1 as no substantial change, 0.1 to 0.25 as a small change and 0.25 or more as a substantial change. It comes from credit scoring and ignores sample size and bin count, so it is not a validated limit for our data (du Pisanie et al., 2023).

PSI has clear blind spots. Each statistic is checked alone, so it misses a change in how features relate to one another. It is blind to concept drift: if the images look the same but what they mean has changed, PSI stays flat. It shows that the inputs moved, not whether accuracy fell. It is also noisy on small samples. Yurdakul and Naranjo show that a rescaled PSI follows a chi-square distribution, which gives an expected value of about $(B-1)(1/n + 1/m)$ with no real drift. I derived that formula from the result and checked it by simulation: it holds at 200 or more live cases (0.066 predicted, 0.068 simulated). At 50 live cases against 431 training images, it predicts 0.20, but the simulation with our 10 bins and floor averaged 0.25, and 1 run in 20 passed 0.60. So 50 cases can cross the "substantial" line through chance alone, and the rule of thumb should not be used at that size. A low value also misses a problem that lives in one subgroup, one clinic or a few rare bad images. In addition, our reference is clean digital training data and the live input is phone photos, so we expect a high PSI from day one; that would reflect skew we already know about, not a change over time. Status today: `reference.json` on `main` still says `pending`, so the page shows live numbers without PSI scores, and it displays values rather than raising alerts. Concept drift will need labels, so we would watch reviewer corrections (`E10-01`) and any lab results, and accept that these arrive late and only for some cases.

## 12. How can somebody attack a data path?

**Poisoning** adds or changes training examples so the model learns something wrong. It requires write access to training data at some point: the source, the download path, team storage or a contribution channel. **Label flipping** is a cheap form of poisoning that changes only labels, so the images still look fine. It requires access to the labels or manifest, or a role as annotator, and it is effective on a small set: flipping just 13 of our 431 training records would alter 3% of the set, and flipping the TB labels to normal pushes directly toward false reassurance. **Backdoor triggers** add a small pattern to training images along with a chosen label, so the model acts normally until an input carries that pattern. A backdoor needs two kinds of access: to training data or a supplied model, and later to the inputs at use. It may also arrive through pretrained weights, so the attacker never touches our data. Normal accuracy stays high, so clean test sets will not reveal it. **Provenance forgery** presents data as coming from somewhere it did not, or falsifies its history. It requires control of a mirror, a re-upload or metadata such as a data card, or of the manifest. Our Montgomery images come from a third-party Kaggle copy, so this path is real for us.

Defences should match the threat and our real exposure. Against outside tampering after download, which is cheap to guard against and very plausible, we have SHA-256 per file, a dataset version hash logged with each run, and manifests under pull-request review, so any change shows up in a diff. A hash proves the data has not changed since it was recorded, not that it was correct at the start. So provenance needs a separate check against the source. For Montgomery we compared sizes, names and label counts with the NLM description and took the readings directly from NLM. Against label flips, we check each label against its written reading and review the worst errors (ML-EVAL-04), and a locked external set from another site reveals broad damage to accuracy. The risk of poisoning through live use is closed by design: there is no automatic retraining (TRD 9.11), so reviewer feedback cannot enter the model on its own. If that ever changes, feedback would need its own validation and review.

At the model and artifact stage, the threat is a swapped model or thresholds file (H7). The current defence is a SHA-256 binding between the two, which catches accidents. It will not stop anyone who can change both files, so ECDSA signature checks on the phone (ADR-11, NFR-SEC-06) are what close that gap, and they are not built yet. Backdoors are the least likely threat and the hardest to rule out. A small team using public data should use trusted sources for weights, pin dependencies and use heatmap sanity checks on failures (ADR-9), and state that a backdoor has not been excluded. Spectral analysis or full data audits would cost a lot, with weak assurance on 431 images and few obvious attackers, so they are not proportionate now. Before any real pilot, with real patients and a stronger motive, this judgement should be revisited.

## 13. What does Act 843 require of a pipeline that stores personal data?

The Act's section 17 sets out eight principles, and these are the ones that matter most here. **Lawful basis:** personal data needs prior consent, unless a listed ground applies, such as a legal duty or a legitimate interest (s.20(1)). Health data is special personal data, and its processing is prohibited unless it is necessary or the person consents (s.37(1) and (2)). **Minimisation:** the purpose must be "necessary, relevant and not excessive" (s.19). **Purpose limitation:** data is collected for a purpose that is "specific, explicitly defined and lawful" (s.22), the person is made aware of it (s.23) and later processing stays compatible with it (s.25). **Retention:** data is kept no longer than necessary, with exceptions that include research records, which must still be protected (s.24, s.65). **Rights:** a person can object and processing must then stop (s.20(2) and (3)), can get access to their data (s.35), can ask for correction or deletion (s.33), can give written notice to stop processing that is likely to cause unwarranted damage or distress, with a written reply due within 21 days (s.39), and can require that a significant decision is not based solely on automatic processing (s.41). Controllers also register with the Commission (s.27) and must notify security compromises (s.31). Act 843 was still the law in the latest reporting I could find (March to May 2026), although a bill to replace it has been proposed, so check this before relying on it. This is my reading as a student, not legal advice, and TRD 11.4 sends the final questions to the ethics committee and the Commission.

Registration, notices and consent wording are paperwork, but the others shape the design and are costly to add later. Minimisation decides the schema: no names, phone numbers or national IDs, an age band rather than a birth date and no image by default, all enforced by database checks and a list of allowed fields (NFR-PRIV-01, ADR-10). Purpose limitation decides whether images could ever become training data, so consent needs a stored scope (the planned `consents` table), not a yes or no. Reusing clinic data for training would then be a deliberate decision, not a side effect. Retention needs a date on every record and an automatic job, and our own 12-month default (NFR-PRIV-04) goes further than the research exception requires. Section 41 supports the human-in-the-loop design, in which the app never closes a case by itself (TRD 12.1). Rights create the hardest conflict. The `inferences` and `audit_log` tables reject every update and delete through database triggers, which is right for accountability and for keeping every result's versions, but the TRD promises deletion within 30 days of a request. Pseudonymous IDs add a second problem: with no name stored, we need a way to find one person's records, such as a case code given to the patient. Both need solving in `E12-03` before any real patient data arrives, for example through a controlled erase path that leaves a non-identifying audit trail. The slice itself holds only test screenings from public images, so this is not a live breach, but it is a design debt.

## 14. What else did I learn in this cycle?

A good ranking score does not mean a usable decision point. AUROC transferred reasonably from Shenzhen validation (0.922) to Montgomery (0.887, 95% CI 0.830 to 0.938), yet sensitivity at the same threshold fell from 0.918 to 0.702. Before this cycle I would have read the AUROC and felt safe. Now I treat a threshold as something tied to a site, and I see the benefit of a safer rule. ADR-20 widened the middle zone, which on the validation split puts 42.6% of images in Not sure. That is the honest cost of fail-safe design on a model that is not yet calibrated: a safe tool can still be a burden if it sends almost half of all cases to Not sure. The new thresholds also have no external figure, because Montgomery is spent for this run and we refuse to score it again. Not knowing is uncomfortable, but it is better than a figure that has been contaminated.

Discipline has to live in code and in decisions made in advance, because good intentions lose under deadline pressure. Examples: the median-seed rule was written before any test score was seen; `evaluate.py` claims a set before reading it, and when a first attempt crashed, no result had been computed or shown; the pipeline stops and writes nothing when a batch check fails. Failures taught more than successes. Shape augmentation changed nothing, which pointed us to the texture finding. The 64-bit dHash failure showed why a batch check is worth having. A careful word-list rule turned out to exclude 16 images because it did not know one abbreviation. Writing these down, in data cards and ADRs, made weak results useful rather than embarrassing. I also learned that a small name matters: the split still called `test` in the files is not a test set, and renaming it would change the dataset hash, so the correction had to live in the documentation.

Most of the difficult work was not modelling. Licence terms decided what could enter a public repository and where the model file could be hosted. A course deadline forced a scope change that needed an ADR. Serving headers such as COOP and COEP, which seem unrelated to machine learning, decided whether the model could load at all. Ownership mattered as well: thresholds, hosting and the monitoring reference each had one named owner and one handoff document, and the monitoring reference still shows `pending`. A feature is not finished when the code merges, only when the data that it depends on has arrived. Finally, the hardest legal conflict, deletion against an append-only record, appeared only when I read the Act against the actual schema, which is a reason to do that review early and not at the end.
